Home improvement permits in North Port & Venice: a newcomer’s primer

New house, new projects: a shed, a screen enclosure, a fence around the pool, a new water heater, hurricane shutters. In Florida, much of that work needs a building permit — and which office issues it depends on where your home sits. Here’s how to figure out who to call, what changed in 2026, and how to protect yourself when you hire a contractor.

Educational overview only, not legal or construction advice. Details below come from the City of North Port Permitting page, the City of Venice Building Department, Florida Statutes §489.103, and the Florida DBPR. Rules, thresholds and forms change — always confirm with the building department for your address before you start work. We don’t rank or recommend specific contractors.

Step one: which building department is yours?

Your mailing address can be misleading. A “Venice” or “North Port” ZIP code doesn’t always mean the city issues your permits.

  • Inside North Port city limits — City of North Port Development Services (Building Department), 4970 City Hall Blvd. Permits are applied for through the city’s Accela Citizen Access portal (free account required).
  • Inside Venice city limits — City of Venice Building Department at City Hall, 401 W. Venice Ave. The city says it no longer accepts paper applications; permits go through its eTRAKiT portal.
  • Unincorporated Sarasota County (many “Venice” addresses outside the city, plus Nokomis and parts of Englewood) — Sarasota County Planning and Development Services at scgov.net.

Not sure? Look up your parcel on the Sarasota County Property Appraiser site, which shows the taxing jurisdiction, or call the city building department and ask.

What generally needs a permit

The City of Venice’s wording is a good summary of Florida practice: a permit is required to construct, enlarge, alter, repair, move, demolish, or change the occupancy of a building or structure, or to install, alter, repair or replace any electrical, gas, mechanical or plumbing system. Common newcomer projects that usually fall into that bucket:

  • Water heater or A/C replacement (plumbing / mechanical)
  • Roof replacement, windows, exterior doors, and hurricane shutters
  • Pools, spas, and the required pool barrier or fence
  • Screen enclosures (lanais and pool cages) and elevated decks
  • Sheds above the size your jurisdiction exempts
  • Generators, new circuits, and solar

New in 2026: the under-$7,500 exemption (HB 803)

The City of North Port says that under CS/CS/HB 803, effective July 1, 2026, owners of single-family homes (and their contractors) are exempt from needing a building permit for work valued at less than $7,500 — but with big exceptions. Per North Port, a permit is still required, regardless of cost, for:

  • Electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and gas work
  • Structural work — North Port lists railings, guards, pool barriers/fences, windows and doors, siding, soffits, roofing, elevated decks, structural screen enclosures, and sheds (detached sheds of 50 sq ft or less don’t need a permit)
  • Any work on a property partly or fully within a flood hazard area as defined by the Florida Building Code — see our flood zones primer

North Port also says projects can’t be split into smaller pieces to stay under the threshold, and that you request the exemption in writing (with the contract or other documentation of the work and its value) rather than just skipping the paperwork. Venice and Sarasota County apply the same state law through their own procedures — ask them how to request it.

North Port also describes a second HB 803 exemption for temporary flood panels and barriers on homes outside a Special Flood Hazard Area, installed by a licensed contractor and meeting zoning, drainage, easement and setback rules. Check the city page for the full conditions.

Doing it yourself: the owner-builder exemption

Florida law (§489.103(7)) lets owners act as their own contractor on their own one- or two-family home, as long as it’s for their own use and not offered for sale or lease. Key points from the statute:

  • You must personally appear, sign the permit application, and sign the state’s owner-builder disclosure statement.
  • You must directly supervise the work yourself, and you can’t hire unlicensed people to do work that requires a license.
  • If you sell or lease the home within a year of finishing, the law presumes you built it for sale or lease — which can create problems.

Owner-builder permits make you legally and financially responsible for the job. Read the disclosure carefully before you sign it.

Hiring a contractor: check the license

  • Verify state-certified and registered contractors with the Florida DBPR’s Verify a License tool. Make sure the license matches the company name on your contract.
  • Ask who will pull the permit. A licensed contractor normally pulls it in their own name — be cautious if someone asks you to pull an owner-builder permit for work they’re doing.
  • Get a written contract with the scope, price, payment schedule, and start/finish dates. Avoid large upfront cash payments.
  • For larger jobs, Florida’s construction lien law may require a Notice of Commencement to be recorded with the Clerk of Court before work starts. Ask your building department or contractor whether your job needs one.
  • Suspected unlicensed activity can be reported to DBPR.

Don’t forget the HOA

A city or county permit doesn’t mean your HOA or condo association has approved the project. Many communities require architectural review for fences, sheds, paint colors, screen enclosures, and landscaping — often before you apply for the permit. See HOA first month.

Buying a home? Ask about open permits

Before closing, it’s common to check for open or expired permits and unpermitted additions — for example, an enclosed lanai or a converted garage. You can search permit history in the city portals above, and a local agent or title company can help you ask the right questions. Venice notes that a permit becomes invalid if work doesn’t start within six months of issuance, so an old “open” permit can become your problem after you buy.

After a storm

North Port publishes a Hurricane and Tropical Storm Recovery Permitting Guide explaining which emergency repairs can be made without a permit and which permits may have fees waived after a declared emergency. Bookmark it with your hurricane prep plan, and be extra careful about unlicensed “storm chasers” after a hurricane.
