Snowbird & seasonal rentals in Venice and North Port

If you spend winters on Florida’s Gulf Coast, Venice and North Port are common bases: Venice for the beach and island, North Port for more inland neighborhoods. This page is a planning guide for a seasonal rental — timing, how lease length affects tax, what to ask a landlord, and how hurricane season fits a winter stay.

Educational overview only, not tax, legal, or landlord-tenant advice, and not a list of available rentals or prices. We don’t publish occupancy figures or “typical” rents because they change. Tax rules below come from the Florida Department of Revenue’s sales and use tax page, section 212.03, Florida Statutes, and the Sarasota County Tax Collector’s Tourist Development Tax overview. Confirm current rates and how they apply to your lease with those offices, and confirm zoning and house rules with the city or the landlord, before you rely on any number here.

Timing a winter stay

Many seasonal visitors look for Venice or North Port housing during the cooler months, often arriving after the official Atlantic hurricane season ends on November 30 and staying into spring. That pattern is a common planning window, not an official “season” with set start and end dates, and we don’t have an official source for how early inventory is reserved.

Practical takeaways:

  • Start looking earlier than you might for a summer week if you need specific dates, a certain neighborhood, or a pet-friendly place.
  • Get the arrival and departure dates, any minimum stay, and the cancellation terms in writing before you send a deposit.
  • If you might arrive in November, read the hurricane notes below. The official season runs through November 30, and a late-season storm can still affect travel and a rental.

Lease length and the six-month line

There is no official “standard” seasonal lease length for Venice or North Port. The stay is whatever you and the landlord agree to in writing — a few weeks, a few winter months, or longer.

The legal line that matters for tax is six months:

  • Rentals or leases of living or sleeping accommodations for six months or less are treated as transient rentals. Florida sales tax (and, in Sarasota County, the tourist development tax) generally applies, as described in the next section.
  • A bona fide written lease for continuous residence longer than six months is treated differently: the Florida Department of Revenue and the Sarasota County Tax Collector both describe those lease payments as exempt from the sales tax and the tourist development tax.
  • If there is no written agreement of that kind, the Tax Collector says the owner is required to collect those taxes for the first six months. The seventh month and after can be exempt if the same renter continues to live at the same place and tax was paid on the first six months.

If you want a stay that crosses that line, ask for a written lease that states the exact term. Don’t assume a handshake or a booking confirmation for “the winter” is enough.

Florida sales tax and Sarasota County tourist development tax

For a rental of six months or less in Sarasota County (which includes Venice and North Port), two layers of tax commonly apply to the rent:

  1. Florida sales tax plus the county discretionary sales surtax. The state’s general sales tax is 6%. Sarasota County’s discretionary sales surtax is 1% for calendar year 2026, which is why the Tax Collector describes the “state sales tax” as 7% in Sarasota County. The 6% state tax is in section 212.03, Florida Statutes. The 1% surtax is listed on the Florida Department of Revenue’s Discretionary Sales Surtax Information for Calendar Year 2026 (Form DR-15DSS). Sales tax and the surtax are remitted to the Florida Department of Revenue.
  2. Sarasota County Tourist Development Tax (TDT). The Tax Collector describes this as a 6% charge on the revenue from rentals of six months or less. It is in addition to the 7% figure above, and it is remitted to the Sarasota County Tax Collector, not to the state.

Added together, those official rates are 13% on a taxable short-term rental in Sarasota County (7% + 6%), unless an exemption applies. Confirm the current combination before you budget; rates can change.

The Tax Collector also says tourist tax is due on the rent and on other fees included in the rent, and it lists examples such as cleaning fees, pet fees, utility fees, roll-away bed fees, and accidental-damage coverage bundled into the rent. If you are not sure whether a fee is taxable, that office is the place to ask.

As a renter, the practical question is whether the advertised price already includes these taxes or whether they will be added at booking or at arrival. Ask for that in writing. Some booking platforms collect and remit tourist tax for stays they process; the Tax Collector notes that this does not automatically cover every listing or every way a unit is rented. You still want a clear statement of the total you will pay.

Exemptions the official pages describe include a bona fide written lease for continuous residence longer than six months, the “seventh month and after” rule described above, and certain situations for full-time postsecondary students and for active-duty military personnel present under official orders (documentation required). Those details are on the Tax Collector’s new-account page and in section 212.03. This guide does not try to decide whether your stay qualifies.

What to ask a landlord (or the listing agent)

  • What is the exact term, in writing, including arrival and departure dates? Is it six months or less, or a bona fide written lease longer than six months?
  • Does the quoted rent include Florida sales tax and the Sarasota County tourist development tax, or will those be added? What is the total due?
  • Which fees are extra (cleaning, pets, utilities, association charges), and are they included in the rent for tax purposes?
  • Is this length of stay allowed under city or county zoning and under any HOA or condo rules? The Tax Collector tells owners to check zoning with Sarasota County or with the city (including North Port and Venice) before offering a unit for six months or less. You can ask whether that check has been done.
  • What utilities are in the rent, and which accounts (electric, water, internet, trash) will be in your name?
  • What is included in the unit (furniture, linens, kitchenware, parking, a garage or storage space)?
  • What is the pet policy, guest policy, and parking rule — especially in an HOA or condo?
  • Who do you call for maintenance, and what is the expected response time?
  • What is the security deposit, when is it due, and how and when is it returned?
  • What is the cancellation and early-departure policy?
  • Where are the storm shutters or panels, who installs them, and who is responsible if a storm threatens during the lease? (See below.)
  • What is the flood and evacuation zone for the exact address? Ask the landlord, and look it up yourself on county maps.

Venice and North Port also have public resources winter visitors use often. A Sarasota County library card is free for residents; if you don’t yet qualify as a resident, the library offers paid non-resident cards (the current terms are listed in our library and parks guide — confirm fees with the library). Trash and utility setup depends on whether the address is inside North Port, inside Venice, or in unincorporated Sarasota County; see the utilities and trash and recycling guides.

Hurricane-season notes for seasonal visitors

NOAA’s official Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 through November 30. Most winter leases start at or after the end of that window, but November arrivals still overlap the official season, and tropical cyclones can occur outside it.

  • Look up the flood zone and evacuation level for the exact address before you book — not just the city name. Venice barrier-island and waterfront addresses and inland North Port addresses are not interchangeable. Start with Sarasota County (scgov.net) emergency-management and evacuation information, and see our flood zones primer and hurricane prep guide.
  • Ask, in writing, who closes shutters, who secures outdoor items, and whether you are expected to leave if an evacuation is ordered.
  • Know where you would go if told to evacuate, and keep copies of your lease, ID, and insurance information where you can reach them if you have to leave quickly.
  • Confirm whether you have renter’s coverage for your belongings, and that flood coverage — if you want it — is separate. This page does not name or recommend insurers. Our insurance basics guide explains the categories at a high level.
  • Follow live guidance from the National Weather Service and county emergency management, not social media rumors.

Thinking of buying after your season?

Some winter visitors decide they want a place of their own for the next season, or a year-round home. If that is on your mind, you can talk through neighborhoods, timing, and a first-pass budget with a licensed local agent — including how a seasonal rental is different from buying, and what a homestead exemption would require if you later made Florida your permanent residence (you generally must own and occupy the home as your permanent residence on January 1, and file by March 1; see the homestead guide).
